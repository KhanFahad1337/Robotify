Buying Guide

Mini UPS vs Power Bank vs UPS

Published 26 Oct 2026 · 6 min read

Search for router backup power and you'll find three products that all promise "keeps your internet on" — a mini UPS, a router power bank, and a full UPS. They're not interchangeable. Buying the wrong category is the most common (and most avoidable) mistake people make before load-shedding season hits.

One-line answer: fixed spot + automatic switchover → mini UPS. Small, movable, plug-and-play → router power bank. Whole desk or PC on backup → full UPS.

What each product actually is

Mini UPS

A wall-socket device that sits permanently between mains and your networking gear. Power flows through it while charging its internal battery; when mains drops, it switches to battery instantly — your router doesn't reboot or even notice. Outputs low-voltage DC (12V and/or 9V), sized for routers and ONTs.

Router power bank

A portable battery with a 12V (and often 9V) output. You connect it to the router directly — no wiring, no installation. It's roughly phone-sized, moves with you, and doubles as a power source you can take anywhere. It's the category our own router power bank belongs to.

Full UPS

The classic desktop UPS: a battery that outputs AC mains power for anything with a wall plug — PC, monitor, lamp. Built to ride through outages long enough to save work (minutes, not hours, under load), plus surge/line conditioning. Overkill if all you want is WiFi.

Buy this if…

Buy a mini UPS if…

Your router and ONT live in one place forever and you never want to think about outages. Mains cut → battery takes over → nothing reboots. Ideal for families, home offices, and shops where the WiFi must never blink.

Buy a router power bank if…

You rent, you move rooms, you want something in your bag when travelling, or you prefer connecting backup the moment a cut starts rather than wiring a permanent inline device. It's also the cheaper entry point — and the fastest way to be ready for the next load-shedding block.

Buy a full UPS if…

The goal is your computer, not just connectivity. Editing files through outages, keeping a desktop alive for a few extra minutes to save and shut down, or protecting sensitive equipment from surges — that's the full UPS's territory. WiFi can be a bonus outlet, not the reason to buy.

Three mistakes to avoid

  1. Using a phone power bank. 5V USB ≠ 9V/12V DC. It won't run a normal router, and hack adapters can damage it. See the FAQ below.
  2. Sizing by mAh alone. Convert to watt-hours and check your router's watts — the full method is in backup hours explained.
  3. Backing up only the router. If your ONT is a separate box and it dies at cut time, your router has nothing to talk to. Check the label on both boxes — here's how — and back up whatever needs power.

The bottom line

None of these products is "best" — each wins a different job. Mini UPS for permanent automatic coverage, router power bank for portable plug-and-play, full UPS for the whole desk. FAQ

Backup Options, Answered

Can I use a phone power bank to run my router?

Not directly. Phone power banks output 5V over USB, while most routers need 9V or 12V DC through a barrel connector. Unless the router is explicitly USB 5V powered, a phone bank won't start it — you need a 12V output power bank or a mini UPS.

Does a mini UPS protect against power surges?

Most mini UPS units focus on battery backup rather than surge suppression. For surge protection you still want a quality adapter or surge protector at the wall. A mini UPS does keep your gear running cleanly through outages and the fluctuations around them, but check the specific model's specs if surge protection is a requirement.

Can one mini UPS run both the ONT and the router?

Yes, if the total load stays within its output rating — many mini UPS units offer multiple DC rails (for example 12V and 9V) precisely so an ONT and router can share one unit. Add both devices' wattages first and confirm against the model's limit.

How is a mini UPS different from a full UPS?

A mini UPS outputs low-voltage DC (9V/12V) and is built for small devices like routers and ONTs — it is compact and efficient for that job. A full UPS outputs AC mains power and is meant for computers, monitors and other plug-in equipment — heavier, costlier, and with shorter runtime once you load a desktop on it.
