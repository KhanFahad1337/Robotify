Compatibility Guide

Check Your Router's Voltage

Published 26 Oct 2026 · 5 min read

Every router has exactly two numbers that decide what backup power it can use: voltage and amps, printed on a label. Reading them takes two minutes and saves you from buying something that won't turn on — or worse, something that shortens your router's life. Here's the whole process.

The rule to memorise: voltage must match exactly (12V needs 12V); amperage from the supply may be equal or higher than the device asks for. Never higher voltage. Ever.

The 2-minute check

  1. Flip the router overLook at the sticker on its underside. You're hunting for a line like Input: 12V ⎓ 1A (some say 9V ⎓ 0.6A, or 5V ⎓ 2A). The first number — volts — is the one that must match.
  2. Router label unreadable? Read the adapterThe black power brick between wall and router prints its output: Output: 12V ⎓ 1A. That's what your router has been living on — copy it.
  3. Check the second box tooIf your ONT/modem is separate from the router, it has its own label. Two boxes can have two voltages. Photograph both before buying any backup.
  4. Note the connector sizeAlmost all routers use the common barrel plug (5.5 × 2.1mm), but a photo of the plug end lets us confirm compatibility in one glance on WhatsApp.

What the numbers actually mean

Volts (V) is the pressure the device expects. Match it exactly — 12V gear fed 9V won't start properly, and 9V gear fed 12V can cook its electronics. This is the compatibility line nothing can negotiate around.

Amps (A) is how much current the device draws. When it's on an adapter (or backup unit), that rating describes the maximum the supply can offer. Your router only sips what it needs. So:

  • 12V 2A supply on a 12V 1A router → fine (headroom, runs cooler)
  • 12V 1A supply on a 12V 2A router → no (supply overwhelmed, may overheat or reset)
  • 9V supply on 12V router → no

And the number that ties it all together: watts = volts × amps. A 12V ⎓ 1A router is a 12W worst-case load — exactly what you plug into the runtime formula in backup hours explained.

The USB exception

A small number of routers and travel ONTs run off 5V USB — the same port as your phone. If your label says 5V, a plain USB power bank (with a decent cable) actually works. Everything else needs proper 9V/12V DC, which phone banks don't provide directly — a common trap covered in mini UPS vs power bank vs UPS.

Double-box setups: check both

In many Karachi homes the ISP's ONT sits near the wall and the WiFi router sits elsewhere. Both die at cut time, so both need backup — and their voltages are sometimes different (ONT 12V, router 9V, for example). Before buying, confirm your backup offers the right rail(s) for both. Multi-rail mini UPS units exist for exactly this reason; a single-12V power bank fits only when both boxes match.

FAQ

Voltage Questions, Answered

Can I use a 12V 2A adapter on a router that says 12V 1A?

Yes. Voltage must match exactly (12V), but the adapter's amperage is a maximum supply rating, not a demand. A 2A adapter on a 1A router simply has headroom — the router only draws what it needs. The reverse (12V 1A adapter on a 2A router) is the dangerous mismatch.

My router label is faded or missing — what do I do?

Check the power adapter's label instead — it prints the OUTPUT voltage and current the router was shipped with. If that's unreadable too, search the router's model number (also on its label) plus "power adapter" online, or send us a photo of the router on WhatsApp and we'll identify it for you.

What if my router is 9V, not 12V?

Then it needs 9V backup specifically — feeding 9V gear from a 12V source can damage it. Look for a backup unit with selectable or dedicated 9V output, or a mini UPS with a 9V rail. Never assume 12V "works anyway".

Does the ONT need the same voltage as the router?

Not necessarily. The ONT and router are separate devices with separate labels — check both. If they differ (for example ONT at 12V, router at 9V), you need a backup that offers both rails or two separate outputs at the right voltages.
