Backup Power Guide

Keep Your WiFi On During Load Shedding

Published 26 Oct 2026 · 7 min read

When the power cuts in Karachi, your internet cuts with it — even though nothing is wrong with your ISP. That's because your router and ONT are powered from the same dead mains as your lights. The fix is simpler (and cheaper) than most people assume: keep those two small boxes on battery. This guide walks through every realistic option, what each one actually costs you in runtime, and which one fits your situation.

The short version: a dedicated router power bank keeps a typical router running 4–6 hours per cut, needs no installation, and costs far less than a full UPS. If your gear never moves, a mini UPS with pass-through charging is the set-and-forget alternative.

Why your internet dies when the power does

Two little boxes are involved: the router (your WiFi) and often a separate ONT or modem from your ISP. Both plug into the wall through adapters that step mains down to 9V or 12V DC. No mains → no DC → both boxes switch off within seconds.

Your ISP's equipment up the street usually is on backup power, so the internet itself keeps flowing. You're only losing the last ten feet of it — the part inside your home. That's good news: solving it doesn't require anything from the ISP, just local battery power for those two boxes.

For most Karachi households whose only goal is keeping the internet alive, the first two rows are the real contest. Let's break them down.

Router power bank: the targeted fix

A router power bank is a battery with a 12V (and often 9V) output built specifically for networking gear. You plug the router's DC cable into it, and it runs the router from battery — no wiring, no tools, no electrician.

  • Runtime: 4–6 hours covers the typical Karachi load-shedding block with room to spare. See the math in how backup hours are calculated.
  • Portability: it's roughly phone-sized — take it to a study room, a friend's place, or on a trip where the router goes with you.
  • Price: far below a full UPS, and you're only backing up what actually matters for connectivity.
  • Compatibility: check the voltage first — most are 12V, but your router's label decides it.

Mini UPS: the set-and-forget alternative

A mini UPS sits between the wall and your gear permanently. Mains power flows through it and keeps its battery topped up; when power cuts, it switches to battery instantly — the router never reboots. That seamlessness is its main advantage over a power bank, which you typically connect when you know a cut is coming or right after it starts.

The trade-offs: it's wall-fixed (no portability), usually costs more, and covers one spot. If your router lives in one place forever and you want zero thinking involved, it's a strong pick. More detail in mini UPS vs power bank vs UPS.

Full UPS: only if you need more than WiFi

A full UPS backs up your monitor, PC and maybe a lamp along with the router. Runtime per charge drops sharply as you add load — a UPS driving a desktop might give you minutes, not hours. If your goal is purely internet continuity, a full UPS is paying for capacity you won't use. If your goal is working through outages, it's the right tool — just a different problem.

First five minutes of an outage: quick checklist

  1. Don't power-cycle anything — let the router and ONT sit; they'll come back on their own when power returns.
  2. Connect the router to its power bank (or confirm your mini UPS battery kicked in).
  3. Keep one phone on mobile data as a fallback; WiFi will rejoin automatically.
  4. If the ONT and router are separate boxes, back up both — otherwise the ONT dies and the router has nothing to route.
  5. Save your work locally; auto-save to cloud resumes when the line is stable again.

The bottom line

You don't need a generator or a full home UPS to survive load-shedding online. Battery backup for two small boxes solves it: a router power bank for portable, plug-and-play coverage, or a mini UPS for permanent automatic switchover. Check your router's voltage, confirm compatibility with the compatibility selector, and the next cut becomes a non-event.

FAQ

Load Shedding & WiFi

Does a router power bank work for WiFi during load shedding?

Yes. A router power bank keeps your router (and ONT, if compatible) running on battery during a power cut, so the internet keeps working as long as your ISP's equipment upstream has power. Typical units run a router for 4 to 6 hours.

Why does my internet stop when the power goes out?

Your router and ONT/modem run on mains power through their adapters. The moment electricity cuts, they shut down — your phone loses its WiFi connection even though the internet itself is still flowing to your building's ISP equipment.

Is a mini UPS better than a power bank for a router?

A mini UPS is better when the router stays in one place and you want automatic pass-through charging (battery takes over instantly when power cuts). A portable router power bank is better when you want a small, movable unit you can also take elsewhere or use while traveling.

Can I use my phone's power bank to run a router?

Not directly. Phone power banks output 5V USB, while most routers need 9V or 12V DC. You would need a boost converter or a power bank designed to output 12V — otherwise the router simply won't turn on, or could be damaged.
